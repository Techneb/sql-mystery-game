For teachers

The Ritz Affair was written for a first SQL course. It replaces a worksheet with a story in which every answer has to be queried out of a real database.

What students practise

Twelve chapters, each built around one construct: SELECT and WHERE, ORDER BY and LIMIT, LIKE, GROUP BY and HAVING, JOIN, several joins with SUM, CASE WHEN, a window function with a CTE, a CTE with a join, LEFT JOIN and the self-join, LAG, and a recursive CTE. The puzzles are built so that the construct is needed: the obvious shortcut (a keyword search, the first row of a sorted list, a plausible guess) is planted with a decoy that gives a wrong answer. The game runs entirely in the browser, on SQLite, with no installation and no account. The handbook covers each construct on a separate example database and can be given to students as reading.

Learning mode

Learning mode (the Investigate button) is for practice, in class or at home. There is no clock, and a wrong answer costs nothing. Progress is kept in each student's own browser and nothing is sent anywhere, so you cannot see learning-mode progress from your side; ask students to show their screen, or to report the chapter they reached. Part I is eight chapters; Part II adds four harder ones and suits the end of a course or the stronger students.

Compete mode

Compete mode turns Part I into a timed session with a class leaderboard. Each session uses a season: a separate copy of the archives with its own answers, numbered from 1 to 20. Using a new season for each group means that answers passed on from an earlier group, or found in learning mode, do not help.

To open a season, add ?admin to the game's address and enter the course passphrase, which you obtain from whoever deployed the game for your course. The admin panel's Seasons section shows which seasons already have results, lets you choose an unused one, and copies two links: the compete link for students and the leaderboard link for you. Students open their link, press Compete, choose a pseudo and start the clock. The Settings section sets the penalty added for each wrong answer (ten seconds by default), the query counts behind the end-of-game ranks, and the telegrams students receive after repeated wrong answers.

The leaderboard ranks finished players by time, penalties included, and shows players still in progress by the chapter they have reached. Results are stored in a Google Sheet that belongs to whoever runs the game's backend for your course; nobody else can read it, apart from the public leaderboard page. Ask students to use a pseudo rather than their full name.

Before you grade

The game is open source, and its repository contains the whole plot, the queries that solve each chapter and the code that generates every season. A determined student can find answers there. Compete mode is also honour-based in other ways: it records the times reported by the browser, cross-checked against the server's own clock, but it cannot stop a student from sharing a screen or a neighbour's answers. Use the leaderboard for motivation and discussion rather than for marks, or grade something the game cannot hand out: the queries themselves, explained in the student's own words, on the handbook's example database or on one of your own.

A few practical notes

Compete mode suits a class that has already met joins and grouping; try Part I yourself first to judge how long your group will need. A debrief that compares two or three different correct queries for the same chapter works well. The stories are fiction: Arsene Lupin and Inspector Ganimard come from Maurice Leblanc's novels, and every other name, place in the data, sum and timetable is invented.

For questions about the game, or to have a player's leaderboard rows removed, write to contact@alephb.uk. What the game sends and stores is described in the privacy notice.
