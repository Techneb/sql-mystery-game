# SQL Noir study (2026-10-25)

Sources: public pages of sqlnoir.com (home, /cases, /faq, /help), its public MIT repo
(github.com/hristo2612/SQLNoir, 1.7k stars; read through the GitHub API, nothing run), the
author's HackerNoon post, search results. No account, no forms. Ideas only: nothing below
copies its text, cases or exercises.

## 1. What SQL Noir is

A Next.js + Supabase site, sql.js in the browser, 1980s noir theme. Six standalone cases,
each with its own small database: 2 Beginner, 2 Intermediate, 2 Advanced (50 to 1000 XP).
English, Brazilian Portuguese, Simplified Chinese. Launched on Reddit and HN: about 30,000
visitors on day one, by the author's account.

## 2. Point by point

### Onboarding (first minutes)
- **SQL Noir.** The home page shows a short headline, three feature blocks (cases, nothing to install,
  XP), a "how it works" section, a "who it is for" section (developers, interview prep,
  students, instructors) and an FAQ. Each section ends with the same button, which goes to the case list.
  No account is needed for the free cases. A case opens on its Brief tab. The tabs are Brief,
  SQL Workspace, Schema, Notes and Submit, and on desktop two of them can sit side by side.
- **Us.** The landing card starts with the red band and the masthead, then Investigate or Join. Inside
  the game, three columns: story, terminal, schema. No account either. The landing page gives
  more atmosphere and tells less about how to play.

### How the first case teaches (the main lesson)
- **SQL Noir case 1** is built to be easy to start:
  - The brief states the decisive clue outright: the witness's description of the thief. It also
    names the three kinds of record to look at.
  - **Three numbered objectives** are a step-by-step path: get the scene record, find the
    suspect who fits, confirm with the interview.
  - The answer is a **person's name**, which feels like solving the case. Matching ignores case
    but is otherwise exact.
  - The data has no deliberate trap. The scene table is about 50 rows of mixed crimes, so
    a WHERE on type or location narrows it fast.
  - After a correct answer: a "case closed" stamp, confetti, the XP earned and a **short
    paragraph retracing the reasoning** (which table gave what). A wrong answer gets a plain
    "not quite" and the student tries again. There are no hints.
- **Our chapter I** asks a lot more of a beginner:
  - The story names Duroc but no table.
  - The objective is bare.
  - The answer is a report id, not a culprit.
  - The student has to guess that Duroc's words are in `interview` and query it by name.
  - They have to read that 02:10 means the 18th, while the chapter's own title says the 17th
    (a deliberate trap).
  - They have to write a three-condition WHERE against 3,016 reports, with dates as
    integers (19120518).
  - Every first query also goes through the strict terminal.
  - That is four hurdles in the first chapter, one of them a trap. It plausibly explains the 28%
    solve rate, set against SQL Noir's three easy, guided steps.
- **Takeaway.** SQL Noir uses case 1 to teach the *loop* (read a clue in one table, use it as a
  filter in another), not to test the student. Our chapter I teaches the loop and also springs a trap.

### Case structure and difficulty curve
- **SQL Noir.** Short, self-contained cases with a new world each time. Difficulty climbs between
  cases (simple SELECT, then joins, grouping and subqueries, then layered joins). The answer is
  checked once at the end of a case. In-between steps are not checked: objectives guide, nothing gates.
- **Us.** One story in twelve chapters, one construct each, every chapter gated by its answer.
  The plot is tighter, the challenge sharper (traps, shortcut checks), and the curve steeper at the start.

### Hints and help
- **SQL Noir.** No in-game hints: a commented-out "Hints & Tips" box sits in the source, so it
  was dropped. The Help page says to check the schema and ask on Discord. Its outside help is a community.
- **Us.** A closed Hint toggle with the construct and the new tables, current tables outlined in the
  schema, the handbook's construct pages, the telegram re-shown. In-game help is richer; we have no community channel.
- Neither gives hints after N wrong answers, which matches the owner's decision.

### Editor and results
- **SQL Noir:**
  - A plain textarea with Ctrl/Cmd+Enter.
  - A small **autocomplete** popup (keywords plus the case's table names, top 5, Tab or Enter
    to accept).
  - Raw SQLite errors in English (translated in zh-CN).
  - A plain results table and a notes tab saved per case.
  - No syntax colour, no formatter, no history, no strict checking.
- **Us.** Syntax colour, Ctrl+F formatting, history, notes, the strict PostgreSQL-style terminal
  whose refusals explain themselves and link to the grammar, a cartesian-join guard, a row cap.
  Our terminal is clearly better. The one thing we lack is autocomplete of table and column names.

### Progress
- **SQL Noir.** XP per case and a rookie-to-master path. Solved cases are kept in localStorage while
  signed out and merged into the account at sign-in.
- **Us.** localStorage progress, query counts, ranks, badges, the case board, a certificate,
  a compete mode with a live board. We have more game systems; they have cross-device sync.

### Accounts and saving
- **SQL Noir.** Optional Google sign-in (Supabase) syncs XP and solved cases across devices.
  Paid answers are checked on the server.
- **Us.** No account, everything stays in the browser in learn mode. That is simpler and better
  for privacy, and it is a selling point. We cannot move a save to another device.

### Money
- **SQL Noir.** Three cases free (the case list shows only two as free, which looks like a site
  inconsistency). The rest unlock with a one-time "Detective License" for life, future cases
  included, a 14-day refund, and **purchasing-power pricing in 13 tiers from $1.99 to $14.99**
  (US $14.99, UK and France $8.99, India $1.99). Discounted classroom and team licences on
  request. No ads.
- **Us.** Free, with AdSense in a single box under the schema and a house ad for the handbook as fallback.

### SEO and sharing
- **SQL Noir:**
  - Keyword titles on every page ("Interactive SQL Game | Learn SQL by Solving Detective Cases").
  - Open Graph and Twitter cards with an image.
  - JSON-LD everywhere: Organization, WebSite, Breadcrumbs, ItemList of cases,
    VideoGame/SoftwareApplication per case, FAQPage, BlogPosting.
  - A dynamic sitemap that lists every case and post, and an RSS feed.
  - A blog of about 25 posts aimed at search terms: CTE, window functions, HAVING vs WHERE,
    UNION vs UNION ALL, JOIN types, COALESCE, "SQL for marketing/finance/healthcare...",
    "is SQL hard", a comparison with LeetCode, and a **"games to learn SQL" listicle** (SQL
    Murder Mystery, SQL Island, SQLZoo, SQL Police Department).
  - A share pop-up (X, Facebook, LinkedIn), plus a Discord, the GitHub repo and contributor
    credits, all of which bring links.
- **Us:**
  - The game's `<title>` is just "The Ritz Affair", with no keyword.
  - No Open Graph or Twitter tags and no JSON-LD on `index.html`. On `learn/index.html` the
    title and description are fine.
  - The handbook's 17 static pages are a better learning resource than their blog. They are
    our version of it, but laid out as a reference, not aimed at search terms.
  - No share button.

### Anything else they do well
- Open source under MIT and open to contributed cases: that brings stars, links and goodwill.
- Language versions widen their reach. **We are English only, owner's decision: not recommended.**
- The audience is named on the landing page (interview prep, analysts, instructors).
- A works-offline note: answers need a connection only for paid cases.

## 3. What to adopt, ranked

The effect estimates are judgement. Item 1 is about measuring before changing anything.

1. **Diagnose chapter I with the GA we already collect** (effort: an hour; effect: tells us
   which fix to make). Of the chapter I openers, how many ran 0 queries, 1-4, 5-19, 20 or
   more (`chapter_queries` milestones); the `query_fail` rules in chapter I; `answer_wrong`
   n=1 and n=3; `chapter_leave` seconds. If many leave with 0 to 1 queries, the start is the
   problem (items 2, 3, 5). If many leave after 20 queries or after wrong answers, the
   puzzle is (item 4).
2. **A prologue before chapter I that teaches the loop and has no clue in it**
   (effort: medium, one small `plot.CHAPTERS` entry or a scripted first screen; effect:
   probably the biggest gain on the 28%). It would walk through running a query, reading a
   result and typing an answer, on something harmless (count Ganimard's reports, say), before
   the case starts. This is SQL Noir's guided first steps moved *out of* chapter I, so chapter
   I's objective stays bare. It has to pass the Shortcut check, and no prologue value may
   appear in chapter I.
3. **Make chapter I's start easier without hints or a fuller objective**
   (effort: small to medium; effect: high). Any of these would do:
   - name the archive in the story ("Duroc's statement is in the interview book"), as story
     text, not as an objective;
   - pre-fill the editor with a working first query, for example the interview
     table's columns, so the first Run always succeeds;
   - show what the first successful result means.

   Check each with the owner: the first two touch the story and the objective's spirit,
   though neither is a hint after N wrong answers.
4. **Reconsider the 17th/18th trap's place in the curve** (effort: small in the plot, plus the
   shortcut recheck; effect: probably high if the item 1 data points at it). SQL Noir's first
   case has no trap. Moving the midnight twist to a later chapter, or making Duroc state the
   date plainly, would make chapter I a pure SELECT/WHERE win. This is a plot decision for the
   owner: the chapter's title and the "Filed Under the 17th" badge depend on it.
5. **A short "how the clerk did it" note after each solve** (effort: medium; effect: learning and
   satisfaction, the "case closed" moment). SQL Noir's reasoning recap is its best teaching touch.
   We must not ship it in plaintext (the JSON carries every chapter), so encrypt each chapter's note with a key
   derived from its normalised answer (WebCrypto AES-GCM), and only a solver can read it. That
   is not the passphrase-encrypted answer key the owner declined. `solution.sql`'s discovery
   notes are the source.
6. **SEO basics on the game page** (effort: small; effect: steady search and social traffic):
   - a keyword `<title>` ("The Ritz Affair: a free SQL detective game"; run the humanizer);
   - Open Graph and Twitter tags with the landing photograph;
   - JSON-LD `VideoGame`/`LearningResource` on `index.html`;
   - `FAQPage` markup over the existing landing-about prose;
   - `Course`/`Article` markup on the handbook pages.

   Keep it in step with privacy.html (no new requests).
7. **Ask to be listed in SQL Noir's "games to learn SQL" post and similar lists** (effort: one
   email, fold it into the outreach drafts; effect: a good backlink). Run the humanizer on it first.
8. **A share button on the finish screen and the certificate** (effort: small, plain intent URLs, no SDK, so
   nothing loads; effect: modest, word of mouth among classmates). Say in privacy.html that it only opens a link.
9. **Autocomplete of table and column names in the terminal** (effort: medium, it has to keep the
   highlight overlay aligned; effect: fewer "no such column" first failures). It must not
   weaken the strict checking.
10. **Name the audience on the landing page** (students, teachers, interview prep) in the
    crawlable about text (effort: small; effect: small).

Not recommended, because they contradict owner decisions or our model:
- language versions (English only);
- hints after N wrong answers (SQL Noir has none either);
- fuller objectives in chapter I (bare objectives; item 3 is borderline, check with the owner);
- a paywall or licence (we are free and ad-funded; the PPP tiers are only worth knowing if a
  classroom licence for compete is ever wanted);
- a looser terminal (strict stays);
- accounts and sync (no account is our privacy promise).
