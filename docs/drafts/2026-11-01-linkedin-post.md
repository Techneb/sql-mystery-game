I taught SQL in Paris. Today my students can ask ChatGPT or Claude for a working query and get one in seconds. What still takes them time is knowing whether the rows answer the question, and what those rows mean for the business behind them.

I wanted something that trains that judgement, so I made [The Ritz Affair](https://alephb.uk/ritz-affair/), a free detective game you play in SQL. Paris, May 1912: someone at the Ritz is missing a great blue stone, and all you have is the police archive. Each of the twelve chapters needs one construct, from a first WHERE to a recursive CTE. Most of the time goes into reading the story and working out what the data ought to show, so you notice when a result looks right and isn't.

[The Clerk's Handbook](https://alephb.uk/learn/) goes with it: fourteen short lessons on a small bakery database, and twenty graded exercises with a terminal on the same page.

About 4,400 people opened the game in October, most of them after Nick Amato (Software with Nick) showed it to his followers. His video passed 800,000 views on [Instagram](https://www.instagram.com/p/DevB79KCKqt/) alone, with more on [TikTok](https://www.tiktok.com/@softwarewithnick/video/7698898589518400782), [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/_WqJYXyifhA) and [Facebook](https://www.facebook.com/reel/1839523360397980/). Thank you, Nick!

To my students, past and present: now that AI writes the syntax for you, how do you check what the data actually means? I'd love to hear.
